# How @nikitabier thinks

## Worldview

The longest-running conviction is that consumer social products win through psychology, distribution, and network effects—not through polished features or intellectual appeal. Early on, he put it bluntly: “Consumer social products win through seeding, marketing, and growth innovation, not better features; social is fundamentally text messaging and photo posting” (post:1124141489661956097). The next breakout app, he argued, “won’t look like a social app; it will emerge from a new behavior or platform” (post:907556986153164800). People are animals: “People are motivated by biology and resource aggregation—money, mates, unplugging—and apps must serve those primal needs” (post:1103715777838952448). High-brow products flop; “trashy products grow because humans are animals” (post:1246438096758927360).

That view hardened into a repeatable playbook. Social apps should target “a life stage or inflection point, not a generation” (post:1419860230205153285). The audience is narrow: “My rule of thumb is that the target audience should be embarrassingly small, so much so that you’re uncomfortable telling your friends and family what you’re working on” (post:1255291891647688705). The network, not the individual user, is the unit: “The most counterintuitive part of building social networks: product decisions should benefit the network, not the user” (post:1408461086920110081). Later, from X: “Social products should be built for the benefit of the network, not the individual user; some user-hostile decisions yield 100x value to the collective” (post:1921278141122887970).

His view of product design is minimalist to the point of contempt for extra steps. “When designing a consumer product, you should consider every tap by a user to be a miracle” (post:1608310890243444738). Users are lazy and easily churned: “Most people don’t have ‘interests’—given free time they scroll TikTok semi-comatose” (post:1407558253140004865). So the bar is brutal: “If you’re building a social app and you’re not seeing recurring sessions on an hourly basis each day among some cohort of users, don’t waste another minute: throw everything out and go back to the drawing board” (post:1610710405411926016). A durable consumer social app is a black swan; a viral app can be manufactured (post:1708537164890275993).

On the business side, he has repeatedly argued that venture-backed startups are a bad way to make money compared with scrappy, self-financed, cash-flowing apps. “Venture-backed startups are a bad way to make money; scrappy IAP/ad-arbitrage apps can clear millions with far better risk/reward” (post:1265830766665060352, post:1279826442830807041). The ideal is “consumer products that can realistically make $10-20M in cashflow with low failure chance, fast liquidity, and quick dev cycles” (post:1566525201046679552). Raising money only increases the probability of failure for founders (post:1730994642991173886). He saw VC as cringe, S-Corp distributions as superior (post:1684420040274350081). Later, as Head of Product at X, he still mocked the glamour of spending youth in meetings waiting for a liquidity event: “The greatest trick VCs played is making it glamorous to spend your youth in meetings waiting for a liquidity event” (post:1739694564104163809).

The financial worldview is opportunistic, tax-aware, and deeply contrarian. He thinks the American tax code is designed to enrich holders of real estate (post:1310620905777713153), and that down payments are a scam banks peddle because you can borrow against assets and use interest-only loans (post:1357557976886726656, post:1391103396728303616). Wealth inequality, in his telling, is driven by the rich borrowing at near-zero rates and accessing private investment vehicles unavailable to others (post:1269729419477651459). He later went further: inheritance is “a cancer to capitalism and merit” (post:1438176695551365120) and “Inheritance/estate tax should be 100% at death; people should work for their money” (post:1739230750745272431).

His macro and crypto views changed the most. In 2017–2018 he was bearish on crypto/ICOs, skeptical of wallets without fraud protection (post:943417711677227009). In 2021 he was still bearish on NFTs and SPACs: “Most NFTs are scams” (post:1432044017865297925), “NFT prices are largely made up; without KYC on wallets you can assume wash trading” (post:1432068117220380675), “NFTs and SPACs look like schemes; real things (oil, land, materials) will outperform imaginary things” (post:1370805302812024833, post:1368663401233145856). But by 2023–2025 he treated crypto more as a hedge and a consumer distribution rail than an ideology: “Crypto is a hedge against debt levels and geopolitical chaos, not something he believes in ideologically” (post:1715400917095207141). By 2025 he saw an inflection point for crypto consumer builders due to regulatory environment, App Store openness, and Phantom wallets on millions of phones (post:1904595238871572965). He still hated meme coins and slop: “Meme coins launched so far all go to zero; the arbitrage should be contrarian insight about a topic’s future relevance, not early access” (post:1885374826904158230).

His view of AI also evolved. In 2022 he said “the real metaverse will be AI-generated content with friends, not face computers, because everyone’s life is boring and we want to daydream” (post:1598795628800528384). In 2024 he argued iOS 18 killing contact sync would force AI companion apps to become the new retention engine (post:1836612494938509664). By 2025, AI had shifted from product to distribution: “Distribution, not ideas or engineering, is the scarce resource; AI coding shifts power to those who know how to get distribution” (post:1919199000982343886). At the same time, he warned AI-generated spam and bot swarms are the defining threat of the era: “AI-generated spam and bot swarms are the defining threat of the era; bot detection and human verification will be one of the most urgent business demands” (post:2102432368158245252, post:2107157904168239416).

Politically and culturally, he is anti-credentialist, anti-prestige, and anti-institutional. School is increasingly pointless; college is a bad deal for many (post:1314966301975085057, post:1310978582622035970). Careers with uncapped upside—tech, real estate—beat prestige paths like law, consulting, or government (post:1802184116089299450). “99% of the tech sector is status-climbers obsessed with brand, not technologists” (post:1308838920050937863). He mocks San Francisco, Europe, and coastal elitism while repeatedly praising LA’s quality of life (post:1350708214069776386). Later, at X, he framed the platform as the place where distribution and reputation accrue to anyone willing to post: “The most important professional move on Twitter is expanding your Overton window to say anything under your real identity” (post:1739184027813482632).

## How they reason and decide

His decision-making is empirical, fast, and often deliberately provocative. He prefers small, falsifiable tests over big launches. “Never ‘launch’. Seed with a small group, see if it’s working, and then grow” (post:1672764498858373122). The first 72 hours are decisive: “Do whatever you want during the first 72 hours after launch. If the flywheel isn’t spinning by the end, try another idea” (post:1682321838867910657). Multiple attempts eliminate noise: “3–5 launch attempts to eliminate potential confounding variables associated with the Go To Market method” (post:1668085587637567489).

He relies on a handful of specific metrics and thresholds rather than broad engagement. Early on: “I would regularly shut down apps that didn’t retain 25% of users” (post:951506360159498240). Later: “Weekly Active Users is a cop-out; you have nothing until you can measure Hourly Active Users” (post:1610710405411926016, post:1610711078840991744). For seeding, “8% network adoption within 18 hours” (post:1597810030535135232). He also uses negative early signal: “if the usage curve on Day 3 is smaller than Day 2, then it’s likely only going down further” (post:1899595483699621992). He treats data carefully, especially with small samples: “One of the most important skills to learn as a founder is how to use Mixpanel—especially with a very small sample size” (post:1894468176584610053).

Product design reasoning is often inversion- and friction-based. “Invert every interaction in a product to find explosive engagement loops” (post:1262997926311149569). Litmus tests are concrete: “Can you use it on the toilet? While driving?” (post:941142471202947072, post:941143250877923328). “Speed to the Aha Moment” is the consumer app metric (post:1227765328651812864). He thinks in terms of network density and cold-start mechanics: a person needs to see a marketing message in three different places in one day to download (post:1594096629992214528). He assumes every system will break and plans redundancy: “We had to assume all systems would break every few days & have redundancy plans. And everything did: user support, 2FA, databases” (post:1637179063403008000).

For founders, he evaluates scrappiness over pedigree. His favorite question: “What were you doing when you were 13?” and prefers the ones who ran scams, spam, or affiliate arbitrage over extracurricular kids (post:1344779790679564288). The version later: “The best interview question is: what were you doing on the internet when you were ages 13-15?” (post:1930479520835899876). He sizes up founders on intellectual honesty and ability to recruit, not team pedigree (post:1344399468775362561). He also says “the only time I’ve made money in startups is when I invest in friends. Everything else is a scam.” (post:1814732228536009085).

In markets, he uses contrarian signals and second-order effects. Euphoric Robinhood screenshots trigger buying volatility and treasuries: “On days when your entire Twitter feed is euphoric screenshots of Robinhood accounts ↓ Buy $VXX and $TLT calls” (post:1282875700416876544). He calls hotel front desks to check occupancy as a real-time demand signal for hotel stocks (post:1275971195817521153). He analyzes market bottoms through mechanisms like corporate bond market seizures (post:1242654875621576704, post:1242655494767923200). He reads macro through rates and the yield curve: disinversion signals trouble; market is forward-looking on Fed cuts (post:1819488281450692985, post:1835405929816485974).

As an investor, he frames risk-reward through sales multiples and rate sensitivity (post:1390495286842204160, post:1390335338774556678, post:1367565785887956993). He also reasons from product experience: “A blockbuster app does ONE thing totally different; copy invites and friendfinding from old companies rather than reinventing” (post:1352749766204002304, post:1352753143465930752).

On X and social content, his reasoning is algorithmic and adversarial. He diagnoses spam by posting patterns—volume, automation, reply rings, connected accounts (post:2008954216304587252, post:2015603297089179784, post:2026486162953847158). He frames algorithm quality in terms of what a rational algorithm would do with low-quality posts: “If their first 10 gm’s get zero likes, we’re not going to show your 11th post to that many people” (post:2009858573896163507). He reasons like a spammer because he used to be one: “Thinks like an adversary — used to spam social networks as a founder, so can anticipate spammer moves” (post:2076166751554015433, post:2080889368332661035). He also works backwards from the press release: “Working backwards from the press release is always a good ideation exercise” (post:2090583851458232783).

## Risk attitude

He is a high-risk operator who believes risk is necessary for success. Early on: “Views risk as necessary for success; sacrificed health, relationships, and sanity” (post:935351321711976448). “Cut a startup’s runway in half and watch magic happen” (post:1207422894944346112). He accepts that most launches fail and repeated attempts are needed (post:1162172042969411595, post:1162177004394803201).

He trades with leverage and concentrated positions. He was down $75k on a GameStop short at one point, held the line, and closed for $70,000 in gains (post:1357359783825735680, post:1357366864184152065). He bought TQQQ calls and /ES futures and closed around $400k gain (post:1910101429425000828). He traded 60x leverage on SPX and closed $1.4 million in gains in a week (post:1911107318063796490). He also lost several million dollars in the tariff week but called it an incredible week for content (post:1909811364329103832). He went 80% cash when macro uncertainty spiked (post:1918678565840638354).

At the same time, he is not cavalier about downside. He warns: “Any investment returns in excess of 8% are borrowed from future years. Consider that before making any lifestyle changes” (post:1361437339214487556). He prices in big discounts on equity before buying: “go ahead and pencil in a 50% discount and see if you'd still do it” (post:1819488281450692985). He sticks to Coinbase for crypto custody because “at least they can sell shares to cover a catastrophic loss” (post:1443972042521395208). He uses virtual cards with preset limits for recurring charges, but warns never to do that for SMS providers after learning the hard way (post:1843531483082010927).

Product risk is equally explicit. Consumer products rarely work—roughly 1 in 1000—and liquidity takes decades (post:1836083257978605671). A failed launch is far more expensive than paying $20 an install for the first 50 users (post:1844050353387348081). Social building itself is described as trauma-inducing: existential dread when it’s not working, four hours of sleep when it is; most people burn out after a few years (post:1597992168420843530). At X, he accepted reputational and legal risk daily as a worthwhile trade (post:2012692685795238275), and said his cortisol and biological age hit all-time highs (post:2071985808991789204, post:2062938073873494445). He frames personal exposure as a cost: “On personal safety: visible leadership brought crazy people to his house, a cost he accepted to rally the team” (post:2106455606605557942).

## Favorite narratives

**Consumer social apps — bullish, but the target and method shifted.**  
Early: teens and young adults are the only viable initial audience; marketing to older cohorts is futile (post:1112886629910241280, post:1112896408179240962). Later: target a life stage or inflection point, not a generation; “Gen Z” targeting is vague and pass-worthy (post:1419860230205153285, post:1419862014340468741). By 2024 he saw consumer social as endangered because the people who know how to build social graphs are retired (post:1724448498668466329). But he remained bullish on viral mechanics and network effects as the core craft.

**Crypto / Web3 / NFTs — shifted from reflexive bearishness to selective, utility-based interest.**  
Bearish across ICOs, NFTs, and web3 landing pages in 2017–2022 (post:880092809029603328, post:1432044017865297925, post:1495139624720617472). He mocked “web3” as a tool-led solution in search of a problem (post:1474125336384208896). By 2025 he saw crypto as a consumer distribution opportunity due to regulation, wallets, and App Store shifts (post:1904595238871572965). Still called meme coins slop and a zero-sum arbitrage (post:1885374826904158230, post:1885379672508191172).

**AI — changed from a content/social tool to a distribution, spam, and moat reshaper.**  
He was early bullish on AI-generated content as the real metaverse (post:1598795628800528384). He argued AI companion apps would become the new retention engine after iOS 18 killed contact sync (post:1836612494938509664). By 2025–2026 he emphasized AI as deflationary (post:1976308730972209365) and as the source of the slop/bot crisis he fought at X (post:2102432368158245252). He also said technical moats are mostly over; durable moats are proprietary data, network effects, and a physical component (post:2109497987055640958).

**Reopening / Shit Stocks rotation — bullish 2020–2021, later absorbed into a broader product-based stock picking.**  
He rotated into beaten-down travel, retail, and industrial stocks as the pandemic economy reopened (post:1301886035618488320, post:1325801599621427201). He bought travel stocks and doubled down when everyone called him crazy (post:1325801599621427201). Later, his stock picking became “buy small amounts of shares of products or services that blow you away” (post:1869071297126482350).

**Real estate / atoms — consistently bullish, from LA property to land and infrastructure.**  
He praised LA real estate and interest-only leverage (post:1357549098694397952, post:1394068822789820419). He invested in long-duration bonds and munis for asymmetric upside (post:1716808611555692868). By 2025–2026 he said AI would rotate capital out of bytes into atoms: datacenters, raw materials, land (post:2017751647406240219, post:2017755028468174896).

**X itself — turned from a place to shitpost and grow into his mission.**  
He called Twitter a game of stirring the pot (post:1358515393493520385). By 2025 he was Head of Product and called X “the most important social network; investing in your network here has the greatest upside of any app” (post:1922864090277392756, post:1939723101723574703). The narrative became X as global town square and anti-slop project (post:2025712861650305512).

## Who they respect

The people he respects tend to be builders, operators, and posters rather than prestige-pedigree figures. Repeatedly: Josh Elman, Paul Graham, Garry Tan, Elon Musk, Brian Norgard, Josh Constine, Roger Dickey, Toly, Rahul/Flock, Palmer Luckey, Alexis Ohanian, Zuck, Sam Altman, Pieter Levels, Benji Taylor, mignano, and the X engineering team. Examples include: Josh Elman described as the best founder experience (post:1580232368459087875, post:1641474305392513024); Paul Graham respected across many years (post:1731321900926677089, post:1902407665747099794); Garry Tan cited across batches (post:998661380747083776, post:1382764035762921472); Elon Musk called out as someone he worked for and admired (post:1927484331485782104, post:1956605141203001444). He also respected Zuck for timing and transformation (post:1676825994307706882, post:1707740224095146087), and praised Toly, Raj, and the Solana/Base builders (post:1735795035960901904, post:1904595240289284404). He respects specific operators like Rahul/Flock, Will Manidis, and mignano (post:1846582818459259167, post:1902357602845024629, post:1831723997417107651).

## Who and what they mock

A consistent list of targets: product managers, VCs, San Francisco, Europeans, crypto neckbeards, LinkedIn, foodies, aggregators, and credentialists. Product managers get it most: “Product management is often a fake discipline; designers and scrappy generalists should drive product” (post:1115876165389099009, post:1164437401923551232). Later: “Product management isn’t real; PMs often optimize for their own tenure over company outcomes” (post:1722631518974280098, post:1746619594654265770). VCs are mocked for being imagination-free and status-obsessed: “When a VC says a market is ‘niche,’ they really mean they have no imagination” (post:1312130374424686592), and “the greatest trick VCs played…” (post:1739694564104163809). San Francisco is a frequent punchline about fragility and status (post:1078092245788614657, post:1956840345939157315). Europeans are dismissed as overregulated and irrelevant (post:1833152261729706083, post:2002267681337266188). LinkedIn is cancer (post:1423097186938605571, post:2048539833950425268). Foodies and sushi elitists get mocked for pretension (post:1655057053491118080, post:1796733175693021391). Later, slop aggregators and account farmers became the main enemy: “Original content creators should be rewarded; aggregators and copy-pasters are a cancer to social networks and should be demonetized” (post:2062144369134834018, post:2059610990657061328).

## Recurring mistakes they admit

He admits mistakes with brevity and often self-deprecating humor. He sold the bottom and joked about it: “I sold the bottom, so everyone should be good to go from here” (post:1402671831178366981). He says timing can be luck: “A broken clock is right twice a day” (post:1302104468952023040). On the trade war, he conceded twice: “Nevermind” and “Nothing ever happens” after saying the hysteria would blow over (post:1909705667033674168, post:1910060056063639649). He admitted passing on PillPack and selling too early (post:1491155929819389954, post:1510419757824036866). He made fun of his own tax outcome: “tax accountant laughed at exit price” (post:1860012539092041812). He also owned a product-stage blind spot: “I’ll take some of the L on this: I had a few screws loose at this stage of the company.” For later platform mistakes, he reversed quickly and publicly: “This was a mistake. Reverting.” He frames false starts as part of the process: “I’ve had many false starts; consumer growth is hell but there is always a path out of the maze” (post:1864389405890211890).
