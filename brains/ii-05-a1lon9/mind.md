# How @a1lon9 thinks

## Worldview

The throughline is attention. In the earliest notes he is already a market-structure skeptic: options-vault “yields” are not real APY, they hide expected losses (1506947893000916992, 1522358223763816449), and most DeFi yield is ponzinomics that should be replaced by yield tied to actual economic activity (1537918282006638594). By the time he is building pump.fun on Solana, that same skepticism has become a consumer-product thesis: attention and mindshare matter more than protocol infrastructure, and the front end is what wins (1759341294252638646, 1759343859618787451). He flatly declared the fat-protocol thesis dead and said value accrues to apps and consumer products (1764121833429721334, 1777063798350004727).

His core model is that people trade for fun and status, not financial sophistication. Retail onboarding, in his telling, is about dopamine, friends, and shitcoins with people you know (1764421594049565071, 1764789801373343769, 1767913658942947586). Trading is entertainment and a social sport; the real world misunderstands this (1759758562316955939). This is not a later rationalization—it was already visible when he called pump.fun the new front page and shitcoins the new posts (1769015825472860408, 1773838117063335940).

Memecoins are therefore not an embarrassing sideshow to him; they are the product. He argues that if attention has value, then memecoins have fundamental value (1850301046565847533, 1831027455408381988), and he uses a Bitcoin-mining analogy to explain memecoin value: more energy consumed creates more belief and value, like BTC (1844778546834628723). He thinks low barriers to coin creation are good because they force coins to earn attention rather than rely on expensive launch infrastructure (1831660037200060581, 1883237911593979933). Permissionless coin creation was inevitable; if pump.fun had not built it, someone else would have (1886424970122682828).

He is a Solana maximalist with a specific argument: better UX, enough liquidity, and a native asset that pumped with adoption (1885388456823841063, 1882559963241091348). Ethereum, by contrast, lost its last moat—developers—and consumer founders and retail are already on Solana (1851044825069396440, 1871960024651804939). He does not pretend this is a temporary rotation; he says memes will not rotate away from Solana this cycle (1805691373640700001).

Late in the notes, the worldview expands from “coin launchpad” to a full creator-economy platform. He says the creator economy is broken because creators are slaves to algorithms and advertisers, and tokenization lets them monetize directly and reward communities (1942948099854328150, 1885040920669601917). He calls this Creator Capital Markets (1954350320915505430, 1963316363813929427). By 2026 he is explicit that pump.fun is competing with TikTok, Twitch, Kick, and the whole internet, not just other crypto platforms (1963708119839646174, 1966561509930094792). Community is the moat: crypto communities need a home with mass distribution built in (2060410186477572542, 2060410823491641645). Later still he argues that PVP is bad for business long term, and the platform grows when runners run and users have the best experience (2109377290157150286, 2108266106481717271).

There is a clear evolution here. The early 2022–2023 version was more of a structured-product and NFT-Fi trader who cared about oracle robustness, liquidity, and hedging thin NFT markets (1556822283607576578, 1586809474882441216, 1590523609734590464). The 2024+ version is a memecoin platform founder who says NFTs are slower, capital moves faster in shitcoins, and the market is an attention game (1793348138436120987, 1793724836516143311). He also briefly chased Blast excitement (1762209355469099346) before turning on Base as hype and grift (1772340226460889517, 1786485881903222884). That is a genuine reversal in a few months.

## How they reason and decide

He reasons like a mechanism designer. Very early, he drew an explicit line between true risk-free arbitrage and directional trades that require a view, arguing that Jump buying UST was not real arbitrage (1523276390602223618, 1523276730462466049, 1523374138706231296). When evaluating protocols, he starts from whether the mechanism can actually scale, how liquidity is seeded, and whether incentives encourage bad actors to exploit the system (1570698499481825281, 1586809474882441216, 1740484955828855262). Later the same instinct appears in his defenses of pump.fun: if bad actors can always change wallets, then KYC and punishments create false safety rather than real safety (1832872101663129849, 1832903228159308203, 1842693979973812609).

He is also a market-structure trader. He looks at short interest, funding, liquidation cascades, and crowded positioning, and he treats crowded trades as fragile (1590554889149202433, 1590559389152587777, 1594633609868099585). He thinks in reflexivity and narrative: narrative-driven rallies can run far beyond fundamentals, and being early or late is the whole game (1612192712127664134, 1612201280356683776). His public trading advice is built around future attention flow: buy coins where you know attention will flow in the future, and bet on retards continuing to be retarded (1793724836516143311).

Once he goes all-in on memecoins, his analytical lens becomes social and distributional rather than fundamental. He evaluates whether a coin is organic by top-10 holder cluster, holder distribution, social activity, whether it was bundled or sniped, and whether the meme has lasting power (1844130011575620033, 1844131805974396978, 1832882290864656649). He dismisses stories that ignore post-bonding Raydium volume, unrealized PNL, or bot wallets (1877815561461154040, 1877815569988166039). He argues that limiting coin creation only benefits well-resourced insiders, while more launches produce battle-tested winners (1823364292026237117, 1855779120703246744).

He reasons from product-market fit. Pump.fun succeeded because it radically lowered the cost and time to launch: $2 instead of $2,000+, one minute instead of sixty, three steps instead of thirty (1823072046500520361). When critics asked why so many launches were good, his test was simple: the market used it, so the market approved (1823079617277861986). By 2025–2026, his product decisions are explicitly experimental: isolate one variable at a time so you can tell whether it moved the needle (2057576103540130255), ship fast and iterate on feedback (2108267110916907043), keep what finds product-market fit, and remove what does not (2071994270777528782). He is willing to justify money-losing features as user-acquisition costs if they strengthen the launchpad long term (2091935138175197511, 2091941361880571930).

He is highly data-literate but also suspicious of motivated data. He checks counting periods and methodology before believing FUD (1895063378319810997, 1898832438463209511), and he rebuts callout-reward critics by showing how their table manipulated medians and filters (2108618810420846647, 2108632868595613789). His deepest analytic belief may be that attention and belief resolve faster and at higher magnitude than real-world outcomes do (2052510697943130494).

## Risk attitude

His risk attitude is bifurcated: he is explicit about downside in complex products, but he is a degen about memecoin positioning. Early on he warned that options vaults inevitably lose money at some point, leverage can liquidate you many times over, and shorting USDT can squeeze to the upside (1522358223763816449, 1572395581766766593, 1590559389152587777). He also warned that most coins fail, pre-sales have a high rug probability, and apeing every launch should not keep working if the market wants to become mainstream (1760338080651792759, 1762605805931815220, 1791132111883178149).

Yet his trading rules are deliberately high-conviction and high-size. He tells traders to bet more, take leverage, and ask friends for a loan if needed (1762583935408914735); he says the biggest risk is being underallocated to the space you log into every day (1819021516723322919); and he claims 99.999999% of established coins are a trap, so you should bet on new launches with real momentum and community, hold for one hour (1819830095839920156). That is not conservative risk management—it is a bet on funneling risk into the most liquid, most attentive part of the market.

He pairs it with personal responsibility. He says memecoin trading is risky and what you put in can be lost, but that is on the individual, not on the platform (1842692663155589293, 1877815589286096970). He warns about centralized L2s being able to blacklist memecoins and nuke positions to zero (1879038646231429332). At the platform level he admits most crypto companies trend to zero, that pump.fun has taken hits including a hack and endless FUD, and that the bear case is losing product-market fit over many years (2049233391615377522). He also admits that incentive programs as lucrative as callout rewards attract wash trading and fraud, so the platform has to screen for it (2092664010697011706).

## Favorite narratives

**Memecoins / shitcoins — bullish, and it only deepens.** By March 2024 he calls this cycle bigger and says the midcurve move is selling too early (1764006526212284856, 1764778757095936509). Later he calls it a supercycle and says memecoins are here to stay even when their shape changes (1902867774046613733, 2071683173453332584). This is his longest-running and most constant view.

**Pump.fun / fair launches — bullish.** He starts out arguing fair launches and non-predatory tokenomics should be the norm (1743058264294617345), then sees bonding curves as the best mechanism for microcap liquidity (1743555381744308382, 1754382856380449233). After launch, pump.fun is the core narrative and later expands into the everything trading app, Coin Communities, and creator payouts (1767641755082793343, 1942948097237172509, 2060410823491641645).

**Solana — bullish.** This begins as a Solana shitcoin season argument (1747401069678805212), hardens into “Solana is the past, present, and future” (1770464350626803835), and remains constant through later product notes (1974239667248562452, 1983556797136019964).

**Ethereum / ETH — bearish.** Early on he mocks ETH maxis and blames high costs for driving away marginal buyers and devs (1747401565743247570). By late 2024 he says Ethereum is losing its last moat; by 2025 he still says ETH maxis are copers (1831391386312339932, 1851044825069396440, 1884288223301754894).

**Presales — bearish from the start.** He calls the UX terrible and the rug risk over 50% (1762605805931815220), then argues presales are predatory and a local top signal (1766937688530473421, 1769378176613134545). He later frames celebrity meta as better than presale meta (1802021236911882681).

**Token launch mechanics and social norms — increasingly important.** He becomes bearish on KYC for devs, paid KOLs, bump bots, trending, ads, and bundling, and bullish on organic communities, trusted devs, and quality callouts (1836841214022406181, 1836165723925704876, 2110754927802827076).

**AI memecoins / agents — mixed, then bullish, then selective.** He called AI memecoins mixed as early as 2024 (1766962991722115434), turned bullish on AI memecoins and agents as a ChatGPT moment (1848454166428926353, 1861493750029025661), and later argued onchain agents are a trillion-dollar agentic-economy layer (2032472708387160561). By late 2026 he removed the Tokenized Agents launch option because it fragmented attention and caused PVP, while still discussing the sector as mixed (2071994268437082155, 2071995373086806020).

**Base / Blast — brief excitement, then bearish.** He was hyped for Blast shitcoin season (1762209355469099346, 1762239737782575265), but ended up critical of the Blast launch and broadly bearish on Base as hype and grift (1763577656044589501, 1772340226460889517, 1786485881903222884).

**NFT-Fi / NFT derivatives — early bullish, then abandoned.** He once argued NFT perps and derivatives mattered because NFT liquidity is thin (1556822283607576578, 1590523609734590464). By May 2024 he dismissed NFTs relative to shitcoins because capital moves faster and exits are easier (1793348138436120987). This is a real change.

**Friend.tech / bonding curves — mixed, then bearish on Friendtech.** He liked bonding curves but ended up mocking Friendtech/$friend as the meta failed (1743555381744308382, 1786091792623956330, 1786266047613419585).

**CEX listings — bearish.** By 2025 he says CEX listings are dead (1905773051830239474).

**Creator Capital Markets / streaming / Coin Communities / callout rewards / holder rewards / custom pairs — late bull theses.** These are the newer narratives he evangelizes from 2025 onward: streamers and creators should be tokenized directly, communities need a native home, creators and callers should be paid like YouTube pays creators, longer holds should be rewarded, and custom pairs should direct buy pressure to the underlying token (1963316363813929427, 2060410186477572542, 2116972823742668961, 2098819322936177080, 2103576976363528261).

## Recurring mistakes they admit

He is not a person who apologizes often, but he does admit specific mistakes. He said he regretted not buying BTC IV when it doubled (1590121578519744514). He admitted chasing the Blast hype (1763577656044589501). He later admitted that the creator-fees experiment was not sustainable and exposed critical platform issues (2009677442064024063). By 2026 he openly admits the bear case for pump.fun: losing product-market fit over many years of trying (2049233391615377522). He also acknowledges that algorithm changes lag and that rewards already paid cannot be reversed even if later judged unjustified (2108657931134869603). In the voice notes, he has a habit of self-deprecating admissions like “no way I lost all my money on anita max cock coin wtf” and “the market got to my head,” though those exact posts are not cited in the source notes.
