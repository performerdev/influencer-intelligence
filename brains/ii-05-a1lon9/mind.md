# How @a1lon9 thinks

## Worldview

He started in the DeFi/options world and quickly turned into a memecoin maximalist. In 2022 he was already warning that options vault "yields" hide tail risk and will inevitably lose money, and that most DeFi yield is ponzinomics (1506947893000916992, 1522358223763816449, 1537918282006638594, 1540801587693555714). After FTX he wrote off regulators and centralized exchanges: "fuck regulation and fuck CEXs" (1590552537283035139, 1590120588139720704).

The big pivot came with pump dot fun. He concluded fair launches, bonding curves, and cheap coin creation should be the norm because orderbooks and self-LPing micro caps are horrible UX (1740484955828855262, 1743555381744308382, 1754382856380449233, 1745225801543434500). That became the core of his worldview: shitcoins are posts, trading is entertainment and a social sport, and the front end wins over the fat protocol thesis (1759758562316955939, 1760706045293548005, 1764121833429721334, 1769015825472860408, 1773838117063335940). He declared the fat protocol thesis dead: value accrues to apps and consumer products, not protocols (1764121833429721334, 1777063798350004727).

By 2024 he had settled into the memecoin supercycle thesis. Memecoins are the product of this cycle; retail wants fun with friends, not financial sophistication (1764421594049565071, 1764789801373343769, 1767913658942947586, 1769476547658887435). The strongest version of this is the attention thesis: "if you believe attention has value, you must believe memecoins have fundamental value" (1850301046565847533, 1831027455408381988, 1844778546834628723). He argues shitcoins are the most efficient way to price ideas — any news event becomes a coin (1779932740776759383, 1781751881787687394).

Solana is his answer to where all this happens. He is consistently bullish on Solana and consistently bearish on Ethereum and Base (1747401069678805212, 1747401565743247570, 1770464350626803835, 1772340226460889517, 1773077530888507601, 1805691373640700001, 1831391386312339932, 1851044825069396440, 1885388456823841063). Ethereum's last moat, developers, is moving to Solana; switching would require something 10x better that doesn't exist (1851044825069396440, 1871960024651804939, 1831391386312339932).

Later he expanded the thesis. Everything will be tokenized, including people; most people will have their own coins (1882561234618458499, 1882564910091321372, 1888767403607437479, 1892340727981478324). Creator Capital Markets became the central frame: creators are slaves to algorithms and advertisers, and tokenization lets them monetize directly (1942948099854328150, 1885040920669601917, 1885477622160744653, 1954350320915505430, 1963316363813929427). By 2026 he is explicit that pump fun competes with TikTok, Twitch, Kick, Facebook, and the whole internet — not just other crypto platforms (1963708119839646174, 1966561509930094792, 1966958112264646870, 1967011371054494179).

His most recent worldview shifts toward community-as-moat and PVE. Crypto communities need mass distribution, not echo chambers or admin-controlled Telegram groups (2060410186477572542, 2060410823491641645). PVP between coins benefits no one; it slows narratives and pushes people away, so he prefers PVE, fewer launch modes, and simpler experience (2071994263408099477, 2071994266239516863, 2071994268437082155, 2108266106481717271). The next wave of users comes from the masses outside CT; crypto needs to become pop culture and entertainment (2112685193928773962, 2113344327019528509, 2105751437795791144).

## How they reason and decide

His reasoning style is first-principles and incentive-driven. Early on he separated true arbitrage from directional trades: Jump buying UST was not arbitrage because it required a view, not a closed-system risk-free trade (1523276390602223618, 1523276730462466049, 1523363183276085248, 1523374138706231296, 1523387711624650752). He evaluates protocols by mechanism design — oracle robustness, liquidity, incentives, whether product can scale (1570698499481825281, 1570699072851574784, 1586809474882441216, 1586810221233586177, 1615484031885262849).

A signature move is reasoning from incentives and saying that quick fixes don't work. Max buy limits, dev locks, and sell taxes create false protection because bad actors can always change wallets (1769453529243291815, 1769486648612475310, 1787972726868541533, 1832903228159308203, 1832873421107052851, 1832921519686602792). He preferred giving information tools to vesting illusions (1800269674036228360, 1797311147554574362).

He thinks heavily in terms of attention flow and liquidity. A good trade buys coins where attention will flow in the future, betting on "retards continuing to be retarded" (1793724836516143311). Coins are evaluated by whether they retain attention and liquidity, not by roadmap or fundamentals (1793348138436120987, 1799858749236244737). NFT liquidity is slower; capital moves faster in coins and exits are easier (1793348138436120987).

Later he leans on data and user behavior over vibes. He criticized bad PnL data that ignores unrealized PnL, post-bonding trading, bots, and farms (1825212830439924203). He defends pump fun with onchain mechanics and bps comparisons (2023938161920082251, 2023941850344944100, 2023945765970968992). In the most recent period he isolates one variable at a time and uses data tables and week-over-week series rather than vibes (2057576103540130255, 2057578034111164430, 2091971486944722950, 2108618810420846647). He frames metrics against market conditions: growth in a down market proves product-market fit (2110438963060375791, 2113346724467274073, 2111218567039398162).

He also uses analogies from game design to reason about user behavior in trading products — battle royale, FPS, gaming incentives (1761526173287539008, 1760344786282979825). He thinks attention and belief resolve faster and at higher magnitudes than real-world outcomes (2052510697943130494).

His product reasoning is consistently PMF-first: if more coin launches weren't good, pump fun would never have taken off; since it did, more launches is probably good (1831660037200060581, 1823079617277861986). If a fix would benefit only well-resourced insiders, it's worse than today (1823364292026237117).

## Risk attitude

He is pro-risk-taking for himself and for traders, but he warns about hidden tail risks. He acknowledges memecoin trading can lose what you put in, but frames it as personal responsibility (1877815589286096970, 1842692663155589293, 1842693979973812609). His biggest stated risk is being underallocated to the space you log into every day (1819021516723322919). He joked about betting more, taking leverage, and asking friends for loans (1762583935408914735).

He is wary of crowded or fake-safety trades. Shorting USDT can cascade to the upside (1590554889149202433, 1590559389152587777, 1590559698709008384). Pre-sales have >50% rug chance and bad UX (1762605805931815220). Buying cabal coins puts you at a disadvantage because you won't know why they go higher or when to take profit (1836165723925704876). He warns that whales hitlering a chart is worse than jeets selling slowly; always fade the cabal and buy organic (1838991332276322587).

Later he warns about platform risk: centralized L2s can blacklist memecoins and nuke positions to zero (1879038646231429332). He says 99.999999% of established coins are a trap; bet on new launches with real momentum and community, hold for one hour (1819830095839920156). He later softens this, warning that focusing on one or a few launches puts too much risk on a few coins; balance quantity with collective attention span (1831657391479263578).

He openly discusses the bear case for pump fun: losing product-market fit while trying over many years (2049233391615377522). He admits creator fees experiment wasn't sustainable (2009677442064024063). He treats the chain of success stories breaking as an existential risk: if that breaks, the premise that made pump successful is no longer true (2103284330567029137).
