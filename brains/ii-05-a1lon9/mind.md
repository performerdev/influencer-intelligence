# How @a1lon9 thinks

## Worldview
The earliest posts show a DeFi-skeptical trader, not a founder. He repeatedly warned that options-vault "yields" hide downside and eventually lose money, so quoting them as APY is misleading (1506947893000916992, 1522358223763816449, 1540801587693555714). He called most DeFi yield ponzinomics and said the next cycle should tie yield to real economic activity rather than incentive systems (1537918282006638594). After FTX he went harder against centralized institutions: "fuck regulation and fuck CEXs" (1590552537283035139), and he treated MEV as a permanent part of the ecosystem that platforms should accommodate rather than ignore (1520917936621432832).

By March 2024 his focus shifted from derivatives to fair launches. He became convinced bonding curves are the best way to bootstrap tiny tokens and that presales are predatory, slow, and a local-top signal (1743555381744308382, 1762605805931815220, 1766937688530473421). He also declared the fat protocol thesis dead: value accrues to apps and consumer products, not protocols (1764121833429721334). In his telling, memecoins are the product of the cycle because the next wave of retail wants fun with friends, not financial sophistication (1764421594049565071, 1764789801373343769, 1767913658942947586). Trading itself became "entertainment and a social sport" rather than a serious endeavor (1759758562316955939, 1760706045293548005).

The Solana vs Ethereum divide is one of his most consistent worldviews. Early on he argued Ethereum's high costs hurt marginal buyers and dev quality while Solana's low costs pulled users (1747401069678805212). By mid-2024 he was absolute: Solana is the past, present, and future; Base is hype, grift, and high fees (1770464350626803835, 1772340226460889517). Later he said Ethereum's last moat—developers—was disappearing to Solana, and switching would require something 10x better that does not exist (1851044825069396440, 1831391386312339932). Even in 2026, when other chains had their season, he insisted Solana remains the stickiest onchain playground (2104306627943796773).

A deeper worldview is that attention itself is fundamental value. He put it plainly: if you believe attention has value, you must believe memecoins have fundamental value (1850301046565847533, 1831027455408381988). That supports the "cringe is a buying signal" idea: the first time something is called cringey, mass appeal is arriving, so embrace it (1794404584128917858). His trade frame was attention-flow: buy coins where future attention will flow, betting on retards continuing to be retarded (1793724836516143311). In 2026 he still returned to the same model: attention resolves faster and harder than real-world outcomes (2052510697943130494).

Another stable view is organic over cabal. He argued that truly grassroots coins let you assess narratives objectively, while cabal coins leave you not knowing why they go up or when to take profit (1836165723925704876). He told people to stop paying KOLs, buying trending, using bump bots, pouring money into ads, and bundling (1836841214022406181). Pump.fun, in his account, took power from well-resourced insiders and gave any idea a chance (1830719899808760024). That stayed consistent into 2026: farms, cabals, and supply-controlled coins produce bad vibes and don't last (1954883015315771665, 1964013871695311276).

On coin creation, his view moved from "fair launches are better" to a stronger permissionless position: more coins are good. He argued that competition at low market caps means only better content and distribution win, while expensive creation lets garbage succeed (1883237911593979933, 1883257777075655026). He rejected KYC for devs because it adds friction, gives false safety, and goes against crypto values (1832872101663129849). He said if pump.fun didn't exist, someone else would have built it, because people want to trade culture, events, and ideas onchain (1886424970122682828, 1892340727981478324). Memecoins, for him, are a 10-plus-year phenomenon that always comes back (1902867774046613733).

Later the creator economy became the organizing thesis. He argued creators are slaves to algorithms and advertisers; tokenization lets them monetize directly (1942948099854328150, 1921942671788064891). That became "Creator Capital Markets": tokenize creators, streamers, and startups so traders can bet on talent before it blows up (1954350320915505430, 1963316363813929427). He framed pump.fun as competing with TikTok, Twitch, Kick, and Facebook, not just other crypto platforms (1963708119839646174). He also pushed "everything will be tokenized, including people" (1882561234618458499, 1888767403607437479).

Mobile and UX are a repeated conclusion. He said mobile is the future of onchain trading because most new users come from mobile even if volume is desktop (1938617757970444355, 1932930911294631998). He also called onchain agents a core part of a trillion-dollar agentic economy (2032472708387160561). In 2026 he emphasized simplicity, lower learning curves, and growing the market 100x by making onchain trading easier (2087913904563200239, 2095607578021515688). He wants the Pumpfun app to act on any opportunity on any chain, never walling off tokens users want (2105051255416705524, 2109708697609834692). He sees PVP between coins as a negative-sum drag; platforms should push for more runners and higher ceilings (2071994266239516863, 2103284330567029137). Community is the moat (2060410186477572542), and bear-market memecoin activity signals the next cycle (2071683173453332584, 2105386543602905217).

## How they reason and decide
Early reasoning was mechanism-design driven. He distinguished true arbitrage from directional trades that require a view, arguing Jump buying UST was not risk-free arbitrage (1523276730462466049, 1523374138706231296). He evaluated protocols by oracle robustness, liquidity, incentives, and scalability (1570698499481825281, 1586809474882441216, 1586810221233586177). He also used market-structure positioning—short interest, funding, liquidations, crowded trades—to judge risk/reward (1594633609868099585, 1590554889149202433, 1612202174758494209).

By 2024 he was reasoning like a launch designer. He analyzed token launches by distribution, cost to deploy, sniper/rug risk, and whether liquidity was seeded fairly (1740484955828855262, 1760402439398899887, 1763363372211503120). He used game-design analogies—battle royale, FPS—to explain trading-product incentives (1761526173287539008, 1760344786282979825). He kept repeating "study the winners, not the narratives" and said the numbers back the thesis (1765456553597980851, 1774154415626215460). He compared EV across venues directly: launching/trading on pump.fun was multiples higher (1769153280297636019, 1770848522071400851). Quick fixes like max buy limits, dev locks, and sell taxes gave false protection; transparency beat them (1769453529243291815, 1787972726868541533). Discovery was the core problem, and organic discovery was harder to game than filters (1783562998356795668).

His attention-flow model became the core trade framework. He evaluated coins by whether they can retain attention and liquidity, not by roadmap or fundamentals (1793348138436120987, 1799858749236244737). He argued from incentives: if bad actors want to jeet, they will jeet; vesting gives an illusion of safety, so give information tools instead (1800269674036228360, 1797311147554574362). He used a product-market-fit test: pump.fun got users and became the norm, so the market approved (1823079617277861986). He distrusted data that ignored unrealized PnL, bots, and post-bonding activity, and told people to inspect methodology (1825212830439924203, 1877815561461154040). He believed the 4-year cycle plays out almost identically, give or take ten days (1831349897351606753). He framed coin creation as the new airdrop farming: higher EV, especially for small stacks (1851022642947489977, 1851002063377088673).

As a product builder, he advocated building in a problem space you understand deeply, shipping fast, and developing relationships with target users first (1882558167722393608, 1882570288250151234). He favored contrarian bets: being early and wrong is better than being late and right (1923347286471413764, 1944497868972359702). He wanted to grow the pie 10x rather than capture value early (1928502626267599108). In 2025-2026 he emphasized running protocol changes through multiple market cycles, using data and user behavior, citing exact bps, and aligning team incentives with holders by making them hold and lock tokens (2009680442656727534, 2029674449390166153, 2023938161920082251, 2013791026222137671). He isolates one variable at a time, uses what worked before as the first step, then iterates (2057576103540130255, 2057578034111164430). Rewards should be based on volume driven to a coin, not on the Xs it makes (2103149769535164897). He used straight-from-source data to rebut manipulated narratives (2108618810420846647).

## Risk attitude
His risk style is aggressive but alert to hidden tails. In 2022-2023 he warned that leverage can liquidate you many times over regardless of funding costs, and that short squeezes/liquidation cascades are the primary risk (1572394870664306692, 1590554889149202433, 1590559389152587777). He said most coins fail and jeeting is a problem; pump alone can't fix the market (1760338080651792759, 1760402446088884592). He admitted regret over not buying BTC IV when it doubled (1590121578519744514).

In the trenches, he often sounds like a degen but reads as someone who thinks about PvP risk. He said apeing every launch shouldn't remain a winning strategy once a market becomes mainstream; adapt or die (1791132111883178149). He warned that 99.999999% of established coins are a trap, while new launches with momentum and community are the bet (1819830095839920156). He called the biggest risk being underallocated to the space you log into every day (1819021516723322919). He warned that momentum is everything and a few whales hitlering a chart is more harmful than jeets selling slowly (1838991332276322587). He said buying cabal coins puts you at a disadvantage because you won't know why they go higher or when to take profit (1836165723925704876). Feeling forced to "make it" by cycle-end leads to -EV decisions (1877056647257846136). He also warned centralized L2s can blacklist memecoins and nuke positions to 0 (1879038646231429332).

He treats memecoin loss as personal responsibility. He acknowledged you can lose what you put in, but framed protectionism as anti-crypto (1877815589286096970, 1842692663155589293). On the platform side, he accepted that most crypto companies trend to zero and that pump.fun has taken hits, including a hack and endless FUD, but remains profitable (2049233391615377522). He called the creator-fee experiment unsustainable and admitted it exposed critical platform issues (2009677442064024063). He was willing to run experiments that may be wrong and iterate on data (2029674449390166153, 2029685969889234953). Crosschain trading may lose money on every trade, but he treats that as user acquisition cost (2091935138175197511, 2091941361880571930). He warned traders to verify the CA of custom pairs and said wash trading/bot farming of rewards is screened (2108266109224751409, 2092664010697011706).

He is more willing to admit product and market mistakes than to admit he was wrong on a core thesis. Early on he admitted regret over not buying BTC IV when it doubled (1590121578519744514). He chased the Blast narrative hard in early 2024, then cooled and gave a critical Blast launch review (1763577656044589501). In 2025 he called the creator-fee experiment unsustainable and said it exposed critical platform issues (2009677442064024063). He also openly included a bear case for pump.fun: losing product-market fit while trying for many years (2049233391615377522). Elsewhere he says he did not make money from the $ALON coin and won't, calling out lies about it (1882665373931287033). His usual response to being wrong is self-deprecating humor and iteration, not defensiveness, though he rarely concedes a core thesis.
