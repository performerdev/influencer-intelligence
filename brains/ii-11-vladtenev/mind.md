# How @vladtenev thinks

## Worldview
The oldest and most stable conviction is that finance should be democratized. He argues the rich shouldn’t get a better deal, and Robinhood serves many small customers over a few large ones (post:1383243228187484167, post:1383243228938342404). Access should not be reserved for the wealthy; private banking, advisory, and private markets should be for everyone (post:1905369015964553395, post:1967569281295716404). By the latest period this broadens into a social contract: people who own a piece of the future become its defenders—a society of owners, not consumers (post:2003150133375172900, post:2065929194102456363).

Market structure is a long-running obsession. He treats T+2 settlement as antiquated and argues real-time settlement should replace it (post:1356687248746455041, post:1795867521897115850). Payment for order flow, in his telling, benefits customers through better execution and zero commissions; critics misunderstand the mechanics (post:1359322133919195139, post:1367524416465170432). Markets should be 24/7, not tied to East Coast working hours (post:1508876330502303746, post:1678933596889923586). Later this hardens into inevitability: 24/7 markets are coming and will likely run on the blockchain, with settlement in minutes, not days (post:1898177463328686554, post:1988039525097173249).

Crypto starts as enthusiasm—self-custody and wallets are key, and crypto will reorganize the financial system (post:1365023283364589568, post:1526647871898009601, post:1590587236202188800). By 2024–2026 the view is more expansive: tokenization is the biggest capital markets innovation in a decade and will eat the entire global financial system (post:1813311081000866005, post:1973609608892784950, post:1990502364269429223). DeFi and crypto become the infrastructure or backbone of the financial system (post:1808560582662181160, post:1988277941277782381). Prediction markets get elevated into “truth machines”—capitalism applied to the pursuit of truth and the future of news (post:1901703746033426919, post:1991995565950050466).

AI and math are the newest extension. Early on, Mathematical Superintelligence is framed as eliminating hallucinations and producing verifiably correct outputs (post:1800177883748671809). That matures into a stronger claim: AI plus formal verification is transforming mathematics, and “vibe proving” will make human proof review obsolete (post:1992401666906509404, post:1994922827208663383). Math is expected to converge with software—future math looks like writing code with automatic feedback loops on validity (post:1994963677666333024, post:2001485557613105175). On labor, there is a genuine tension: he warns that AI can erode human mastery for convenience (post:1924582379307598255), but he also argues AI will create a “job singularity”—a Cambrian explosion of new jobs and single-person unicorns, not mass unemployment (post:2002156180559573460, post:2011834003444404424).

Regulation is consistently pro-innovation. He opposed regulation by enforcement and demanded clarity for crypto (post:1787595554538786977, post:1793376331423764653). Later he backs specific US policy such as the CLARITY Act and safe stablecoin yield (post:2011622052457783432, post:2035098172700467279, post:2090591694399914461). The underlying view: technology and innovation should make markets more accessible, not be stifled by regulation (post:1356687254551322627, post:1787595555981668751).

## How they reason and decide
He reasons from first principles: reduce complex problems to constituent bits, develop hypotheses, design experiments, debate vigorously, and change minds with evidence (post:1407774347402498048, post:1407774348774035459, post:1407774349575168006). He explicitly says, “We debate vigorously and change our minds when confronted with the right evidence” (post:1407774349575168006).

His operating theory is process-heavy. “Process goals >> performance goals > outcome goals” (post:2003533729080463698). The edge is evaluation: “Run more experiments, pick better, repeat” (post:2019574512124326012). Consistently, he says consensus is a lagging indicator; builders move on first principles with conviction, not complacency (post:2018448321120174340).

He leans on historical analogy to make adoption arguments: cars vs horseless carriages, chess after Deep Blue, Sumerian tablets, Kekulé’s snake (post:1905778953669026023, post:1974191753021251830, post:1991338259864224004, post:1971999438622413017). Later he uses 1776 as an analogy for financial participation (post:2057980041951363152). He also uses symmetry and steelmanning: in a critique of Dario Amodei, he names the risk he thinks is underweighted—economic concentration—rather than just dismissing the argument (post:2016332563879502219).

He demands receipts. “Any announcement of a mathematical or scientific result or accomplishment should be accompanied by the appropriate supporting data” (post:2001830061591454199). That same evidence-first mode shows in earlier threads on sub-penny pricing and settlement cycles (post:1408089396880941061, post:1408089397661102087).

He treats shipping velocity as strategy. The language is constant: “We’ve been cooking,” “the team is cranking” (post:1866207627652505745, post:1950215604117414101). Competition is a race to be won with the sharpest, most hardcore team (post:1866207627652505745, post:1933701899708215740). He also says the best roadmap is built from conversations with customers and community input (post:1968466119234072707, post:1874204170649305111).

## Risk attitude
His early risk posture is safety-first, especially in crypto and customer protection (post:1377737685595348992, post:1590587237426946049). He warns about misinformation and scams and tells people to do due diligence (post:910175111889563648, post:1590587241742880769). He acknowledges shortfalls and risk in trading, especially options, and points to educational resources (post:1274074334001131520, post:1392947600194621440).

Trading risk is framed as a challenge to master, not avoid: investing is like playing the violin—you cannot just learn from a book, you need to practice and put in the work (post:1929678870095212598). He also flags mechanical risks: short selling involves unlimited risk when announcing the feature (post:1972796566248530329, post:1973087653810626908).

At the system level, he worries about clearinghouse deposit requirements and pushes for real-time settlement as a mitigation (post:1356687252315803648, post:1356687253754376193). Later, concentrated ownership becomes a systemic fragility—societies fracture when too many are excluded from wealth (post:2065929194102456363, post:2066505950039527712). He also treats excluding people from AI upside as a political risk that could knee-cap the industry (post:2001782195934134614, post:2016332563879502219).

He does not only talk about external risk. He owns security failure publicly after his X account was compromised by social engineering (post:2080483725209649349, post:2082125660294713761). He also acknowledges product failures directly, as with HOOD Holidays giveaway traffic issues, and commits to remediation (post:2004756962890993834). On private markets, he notes exposure differs from public equity—rights, liquidity, pricing—and says the differences should be made clear (post:2047353331404964341).

## Recurring mistakes they admit
Early on he says he is always improving, an admission framed as a permanent state rather than a one-time fix. Later he owns failures more concretely. He apologizes for the HOOD Holidays giveaway traffic issues and commits to remediation (post:2004756962890993834). After his X account was compromised, he says plainly: “I did not do this well, to put it mildly” and explains the cause and remediation (post:2082125660294713761). The pattern is to admit, identify the system failure, then move to a fix rather than linger.
